Brown Timeout Decisions

When should we use a timeout, and when should we save it? We looked at every FBS and FCS game since 2023 to put a number on what a timeout is actually worth, worked out the best strategy on paper, and checked it against what teams really do.

Every FBS and FCS game, 2023 to 2026 (2026 through week 4) 5,578 games 35,040 timeouts Data: PFF Ultimate
The short answer

A timeout's real job is stopping the clock, and that only matters when we are behind late in the game. For the first three quarters, a timeout is worth far less than five yards, so use one rather than take a delay of game. When we are behind late and time gets tight, call timeout right after the whistle, starting as soon as they have no more snaps left before a punt than we have timeouts. Time is tight when, even if they run out the clock, we would get the ball back with less than about 2:30, which is how long most hurry-up scoring drives take. Teams often wait too long, and so has Brown.

The theory

The math behind a timeout

A timeout does one thing the scoreboard cares about: it stops the clock. So its value comes down to simple arithmetic.

Value of a timeout= seconds it saves× what one second is worth right now

Seconds it saves

When a team is running out the clock, each snap takes about 41 seconds: 7 for the play and 34 between plays. A timeout wipes out the time between plays, so on defense it saves about 34 seconds. In our two-minute offense we are already hurrying, so a timeout saves only about 16.

What a second is worth

Early in a game, almost nothing, because there is plenty of time left. Late in a close game, a lot. Down 3 with 2:00 left and the other team holding the ball, each second is worth about 0.05% in chance of winning, so one timeout is worth about 1.7%.

If they are ahead, their best play

  • Snap with the play clock near zero. Every second left on it is a second they didn't burn.
  • Run the ball and stay in bounds.
  • Kneel when the clock allows. Each kneel-down uses about 36 seconds.
  • Don't call timeouts late. It only gives us time back.

If we are behind, our best answer

  • Only bother once time is tight: if they ran the clock on every snap left before their punt, we would get the ball back with less than about 2:30. Three out of four hurry-up scoring drives finish inside that. Before then, a timeout saved is as good as one used.
  • On defense, call timeout the moment the whistle blows. Waiting 20 seconds and then calling it throws those 20 seconds away.
  • Start once their snaps left before a punt are no more than our timeouts (table below). Before that, waiting a down costs nothing: if they get a first down, we still have the timeout for the next set of downs.
  • Use them on defense before offense: about 34 seconds saved instead of 16.
  • If the clock already stopped (incomplete pass, out of bounds, change of possession), don't call one.
  • Before halftime, timeouts don't carry over. In the last 2:00, use them whenever the extra time could get us points.

When to start calling them on defense

Their snaps left before a punt: 4 before a 1st-down snap, 3 before 2nd down, 2 before 3rd, 1 before 4th. Once time is tight, call when we have at least that many timeouts.

Play clock about to run out

Call timeout or take the delay of game?

When the play clock is about to hit zero, we either call timeout or take a delay of game. Outside the final few minutes, the timeout is the better choice in every situation we checked. The penalty lowers our chance of winning by 1.8% to 4.7%, depending on the down and where we are on the field. A timeout saved for later is worth only about 0.33%. Theory vs. reality

How Brown uses its timeouts

Brown uses 3.4 timeouts a game, a little more than other Ivy teams (3.1). The extra ones go to stopping the clock at the end of halves, which is a good use. How we did it

  • The data: play-by-play for every FBS and FCS game since 2023 from PFF (5,578 games). We start in 2023 because the NCAA changed its clock rules that year.
  • Finding the timeouts: PFF doesn't list timeouts directly, but it tracks how many each team has left. Every time that number drops, a timeout was called. That gave us 35,040 timeouts, and they match PFF's own count almost exactly.
  • Time per play: PFF records the game clock and the play clock at every snap. When the clock keeps running, the time from one snap to the next splits into the play itself and the time between plays. PFF also marks when an offense is running out the clock (four-minute offense) or hurrying (two-minute offense).
  • Chance of winning: we estimate it from the score, how many points the current drive is likely to produce, how much time is left, and how good each team is. The less time left, the more the score decides it.
  • What a timeout is worth: its main job late in a game is stopping the clock. We measured how much clock one saves (about 34 seconds when the other team is running out the clock, 16 seconds in our two-minute offense) and asked how much those seconds raise our chance of winning.
  • The card: for each situation it finds when time gets tight, then applies the rules from the math section (their snaps left against our timeouts, saving them for defense when we need two scores, and whether they can kneel it out). Time is tight when our drive would have less time than three out of four of the 430 successful hurry-up scoring drives needed for that distance: about 2:25 to get from our 25 into field-goal range, 2:45 for a touchdown. When they have the ball we assume a stop gets us the ball where their punt usually lands (about 40 yards downfield, no worse than our 14), and that inside our 35 a stop still gives up a field goal.
  • All numbers are changes in our chance of winning. Going from 30% to 32% is shown as +2%.
  • What this leaves out: timeouts have other uses, like fixing a bad play call or settling the team, and those aren't counted. So treat these numbers as the least a timeout is worth.
  • Why not just compare teams that had timeouts left with teams that didn't? We tried. Teams that use timeouts early are usually having a bad day (they win 43% of the time, against 53% for everyone else), so that approach made timeouts look far more valuable than they really are.
